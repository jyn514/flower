i have a lot of neurodivergent friends. they like to infodump, in many different contexts. sometimes this is good and fun and healthy. sometimes it is not. understanding why requires understanding the difference between communicating and infodumping.

[Dean Barnlund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnlund%27s_model_of_communication) calls communication "the production of meaning, rather than the production of messages". personally, i would describe communication as *goal oriented transmission of information*.

infodumping is when you deliver a lot of information at once, often unordered and stream-of-conscious. it's very easy for infodumping to miss the "goal-oriented part". a goal requires knowing not just *what* you are saying but *who* you are saying it to, and with what intended *effect*.

consider the following scenarios:
## infodumping about a special interest to a friend
usually good! why? you don't need a goal when you're having a conversation with friends.

the exception is if the person you're infodumping to isn't interested in the topic. infodumping anyway puts them in an awkward situation where they either have to ask you to stop or sit through a long conversation they didn't want to be in.

another tricky scenario is when the other person is interested, but an infodump is not the right level of detail for them right now. perhaps they are new to the topic, or perhaps they asked a direct question. if they're still trying to get the "big picture", zooming in to fine-grained details will often just confuse them further.
## infodumping about a work output
depends on context! if your goal is to have a reference document you can refer back to, infodumps are great. if your goals is to get promoted, or to get an interview, or to write a tutorial or guide, an infodump is not the right tool. you need to be *legible*, which means explaining concrete impacts in terms your audience understands. it's not enough to say what's true; you have to also say why it's important.
## infodumping in a blog post
again, depends on context. if you are writing to practice the skill of writing, or to have a reference document, or to share with your friends, infodumping is fine. if you are writing with a goal in mind—say you want to [give a name to an idea](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/) or [communicate when software can fail](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/oldnewthing/20250808-00/?p=111447) or [enter an idea into the overton window](https://jyn.dev/how-i-use-my-terminal/)—you have to be intentional. you have to consider your audience, and the background you expect them to start from. posting the equivalent of a wikipedia article is rarely the most effective way to instill an idea.
## infodumping about a mistake
almost always the wrong choice. if your goal is to apologize, apologize. if you were in an altered mental state (angry, sleep-deprived, experiencing a trauma trigger), you can add at most 1-2 sentences of context asking the other person to mitigate judgement. not all apologies need context; often "i was wrong, i'm sorry" is enough.

the problem with infodumping when you apologize is that it buries the apology and puts a burden on the other person to give you emotional support. the amount of context also implies that you *expect* the other person to forgive you, and [an apology given in expectation of forgiveness is really just a request for absolution](https://bsky.app/profile/did:plc:h2okxbr76w5522tailkxmidq/post/3lvrxmdvb3s2r).
## what to do instead?
be intentional in how you communicate. consider your audience. miscommunications are inevitable, you can't avoid them by infodumping in advance. what you can do instead is make them less likely by adapting your message to your audience, and correct the *actual* miscommunications after the fact, rather than trying to preempt them by considering every possible way the conversation could go.
